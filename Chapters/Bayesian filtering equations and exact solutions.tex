\section{Introduction to Bayesian filtering}
Many systems evolve over time while being observed through noisy measurements. 
Examples include tracking the position of a moving object, estimating the temperature in a room using imperfect sensors, or predicting financial quantities from uncertain market data. In such situations the true state of the system cannot be observed directly.
Instead, we receive measurements that are corrupted by noise and therefore only provide partial information about the true state. This leads to a natural question: ``Given everything we have observed so far, what is our best estimate of the current state of the system?''.

Bayesian filtering provides a principled framework to answer this question. The key idea is to represent our knowledge about the state as a probability distribution rather than a single value. This distribution captures both our estimate of the state and the uncertainty associated with that estimate. More detail on Bayesian filtering can be found in~\cite[Ch.~4]{sarkka:bayesian_filtering} and~\cite[Ch.~1]{labbe:kalman_and_bayesian_filters}

At each time step we combine two sources of information:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the \textit{system model}, which describes how the state evolves over time,
    \item the \textit{measurements}, which provide noisy observations of the system.
\end{itemize}

The model allows us to predict how the state might change, while the measurements help us correct this prediction. Bayesian filtering combines these two pieces of information recursively as new data arrives.

To make this idea more concrete, consider again the drone example of Section~\ref{sec: state space model}. The drone moves through space with some unknown position and velocity. At each time step we receive a GPS measurement of the drone’s position. However, the measurement contains noise and therefore does not exactly match the drone’s true location.

If we relied solely on the measurement, our estimate would jump around due to measurement noise. If we relied solely on the transition model, our estimate would drift away from the true trajectory over time. Bayesian filtering solves this problem by combining both sources of information. The model predicts where the drone should be, and the measurement corrects this prediction. By repeating this prediction and correction process over time, Bayesian filtering produces a continuously updated estimate of the system state.

\section{Bayesian filtering equations}
We now derive the general recursive equations used in Bayesian filtering. These equations describe how our belief about the state of a system evolves as new measurements become available. Recall that in a probabilistic state space model of Definition \ref{def:ssm} the system is described by a sequence of hidden states $\{\mathbf{x}_t\}_{t \geq 0}$ and a sequence of observations $\{\mathbf{y}_t\}_{t \geq 1}$. The model is defined by 

\begin{itemize}
    \item the \textit{initial distribution} $p(\mathbf{x}_0)$,
    \item the \textit{state transition model} $p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1})$,
    \item the \textit{observation model} $p(\mathbf{y}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$.
\end{itemize}
At time step $t$ we have observed the sequence of measurements $\mathbf{y}_{1:t} = \{\mathbf{y}_1, \dots, \mathbf{y}_t\}$.
The goal of Bayesian filtering is to compute the posterior distribution of the current state
\[
p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t}),
\]
which represents our belief about the current state $\mathbf{x}_t$ after incorporating all measurements up to time $t$. This is called the \emph{filtering distribution}. 

A closely related object is the \emph{smoothing distribution}. The filtering distribution uses the measurements up to time $t$, so it answers the question of where the system is now. Suppose instead that a whole data set $\mathbf{y}_{1:T}$ has already been recorded and we look back at an earlier time $t < T$. We can then ask for
\[
p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:T}), \qquad t < T,
\]
the distribution of a past state given all measurements, including those that arrived after time $t$. Later measurements carry information about where the system was earlier, so the smoothing distribution is typically more concentrated than the filtering distribution at the same time $t$. Smoothing can be understood as an update of the past. Once the full record is available, every earlier state estimate is revisited in the light of what came afterwards. The smoothing distributions can be computed by a recursion of their own, which runs backwards through the data and starts from the filtering distribution at the final time $T$ \cite[Ch.~8]{sarkka:bayesian_filtering}. In this thesis we work with the filtering distributions, which are computed forward in time as the data arrives, and we do not develop the smoothing recursion further.

The key insight behind Bayesian filtering is that the filtering distribution can be computed \textit{recursively}. At each time step we predict the new state using the dynamic model and then update this prediction using the latest observation. The resulting recursive equations are known as the Bayesian filtering equations. 

The recursion involves a second distribution alongside the filtering distribution, the \emph{predictive distribution} $p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1})$. It describes our belief about the state $\mathbf{x}_t$ before the measurement at time $t$ has been seen, and it is the intermediate result of the prediction step in the theorem below.


\begin{theorem}[Bayesian filtering equations, \cite{sarkka:bayesian_filtering} Thm.~4.1]\label{thm:bayes filtering eq}
The recursive equations (the Bayesian filter) for computing the predictive distribution $p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1})$ and the filtering distribution $p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t})$ at the time step $t$ are given by the following Bayesian filtering equations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Initialization} 
    The recursion starts from the prior distribution 
    \[
    p(\mathbf{x}_0).
    \]
    \item \textbf{Prediction step} 
    The predictive distribution of the state $\mathbf{x}_t$ at the time $t$ is obtained by propagating the previous filtering distribution through the dynamic model:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq: prediction step}
         p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}) = \int p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1})\, p(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{t-1}.
    \end{equation}
    This equation is known as the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation. 
    \item \textbf{Update step} 
    After observing the measurement $\mathbf{y}_t$ at time step $t$ the filtering distribution of the state $\mathbf{x}_t$ can be computed by Bayes' rule
    \begin{equation}\label{eq: update step}
        p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t}) = \frac{1}{Z_t}\textbf{ }p(\mathbf{y}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_t)\textbf{ }p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}),
    \end{equation}

    where the normalization constant $Z_t$ is given as 
    \begin{equation}\label{eq: normalization constant}       
        Z_t = \int p(\mathbf{y}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_t) \, p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}) \, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_t.
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}
For discrete state spaces the integrals are replaced by summations.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We sketch the derivation of both steps. The full proof can be found in
\cite[p.~55 and 56]{sarkka:bayesian_filtering}.

\noindent\textit{Prediction \eqref{eq: prediction step}.}
Marginalizing over $\x_{t-1}$ and applying the Markov property
\eqref{prop: Markov property of states},
\begin{align*}
  p(\x_{t} \mid \y_{1:t-1})
  &= \int p(\x_{t}, \x_{t-1} \mid \y_{1:t-1})\,
         \mathrm{d}\x_{t-1} \\
  &= \int p(\x_{t} \mid \x_{t-1}, \y_{1:t-1})\,
         p(\x_{t-1} \mid \y_{1:t-1})\,
         \mathrm{d}\x_{t-1} \\
  &\overset{\eqref{prop: Markov property of states}}{=}
    \int p(\x_{t} \mid \x_{t-1})\,
         p(\x_{t-1} \mid \y_{1:t-1})\,
         \mathrm{d}\x_{t-1}.
\end{align*}

\noindent\textit{Update \eqref{eq: update step}.}
Treating the new observation $\y_{t}$ as data in Bayes' theorem
(Theorem~\ref{thm:bayes}), with the predictive distribution as the prior, and
using conditional independence of measurements \eqref{prop: Conditional independence of measurements},
\begin{align*}
  p(\x_{t} \mid \y_{1:t})
  &= \frac{p(\y_{t} \mid \x_{t}, \y_{1:t-1})\,
           p(\x_{t} \mid \y_{1:t-1})}
          {p(\y_{t} \mid \y_{1:t-1})} \\
  &\overset{\eqref{prop: Conditional independence of measurements}}{=}
    \frac{p(\y_{t} \mid \x_{t})\,
          p(\x_{t} \mid \y_{1:t-1})}{Z_{t}},
\end{align*}
where $Z_{t} = p(\y_{t} \mid \y_{1:t-1})$ is obtained by the law of
total probability, giving \eqref{eq: normalization constant}.
\end{proof}

Intuitively, the prediction step \eqref{eq: prediction step} propagates our previous belief about the system forward in time using the transition model. This gives an estimate of where the system is expected to be before observing the new measurement. 
The update step \eqref{eq: update step} then uses Bayes' theorem to incorporate the new measurement. If the measurement agrees with the prediction, the estimate changes only slightly. If the measurement differs significantly from the prediction, the estimate shifts toward the measurement. By repeating these two steps at each time step, Bayesian filtering produces a continuously updated estimate of the system state.

At each time step $t$, we want to compute the posterior distribution of the current state given all observations up to that point, $p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t})$, which represents our best estimate of where the drone is and how fast it is moving.

Repeating these steps over time provides a continuous estimate of the drone’s trajectory. In the special case where the models are linear and the noise is Gaussian, this method becomes the \emph{Kalman filter}, which will be discussed in Chapter~3.

The drone example of Section~\ref{sec: state space model} illustrates both steps of the Bayesian filtering equations  (Theorem~\ref{thm:bayes filtering eq}). The state  $\mathbf{x}_{t} = (p_{x,t}, p_{y,t}, v_{x,t}, v_{y,t})^{\top}$ contains position and velocity, and the GPS provides noisy position measurements $\mathbf{y}_{t}$. At time $t-1$, all information accumulated from the GPS readings $\y_{1}, \ldots, \y_{t-1}$ is summarized by the filtering distribution $p(\x_{t-1} \mid \y_{1:t-1})$. This distribution is the starting point for the next cycle.

\textbf{Prediction step:}
Before the new GPS reading arrives at time $t$, the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation \eqref{eq: prediction step} is applied with the constant-velocity transition model \eqref{eq:drone transition}. The result is the predictive distribution $p(\x_{t} \mid \y_{1:t-1})$, which reflects where the drone is expected to be based on its last known position and velocity. This distribution is broader than the filtering distribution at $t-1$. Time has passed, wind may have nudged the drone off course, and no new observation has arrived to reduce uncertainty. The spread of the predictive distribution is controlled by the process noise covariance $\mathbf{Q}$.

\textbf{Update step:}
The GPS reading $\y_{t}$ arrives. Bayes' rule \eqref{eq: update step} combines the predictive distribution with the likelihood $p(\y_{t} \mid \x_{t})$ from the observation model \eqref{eq: drone observation}. States whose predicted position is consistent with the GPS reading receive high likelihood and are up-weighted, while states whose predicted position differs from the reading receive low likelihood and are down-weighted. The normalizing constant $Z_{t}$ ensures the result is a valid probability distribution. The outcome is the filtering distribution $p(\x_{t} \mid \y_{1:t})$, which is sharper than the predictive distribution since the GPS reading has reduced uncertainty about the drone's position.

This predict-update cycle then repeats at $t+1$, with $p(\x_{t} \mid \y_{1:t})$ serving as the new starting distribution. In this way the full history of GPS readings is never stored explicitly. It is absorbed into the current filtering distribution at each step.

The integrals in \eqref{eq: prediction step} and \eqref{eq: normalization constant} are generally intractable. For the drone model, however, both the transition and observation models are linear and all noise is Gaussian, so both integrals can be evaluated in closed form. This is precisely the setting of the Kalman filter, which derives the explicit recursion in Chapter~\ref{chap:kalman}.
\end{example}


\section{Exact solutions in Bayesian filtering}\label{sec: exact solutions in bayesian filtering}
In practice, the integrals in the Bayesian filtering equations are rarely analytically tractable, and approximations are required. Exact closed-form solutions exist in two special cases. The first is the \textit{linear Gaussian state space model}, in which the transition and measurement models are both linear and all noise terms are Gaussian. In this case the predictive and filtering distributions are Gaussian at every time step, so each of them is described completely by a mean vector and a covariance matrix, and the filtering equations reduce to explicit update formulas for these two quantities. This recursion is the \textit{Kalman filter}, the subject of Chapter~\ref{chap:kalman}. The second case is a \textit{finite state space}, in which the state can take only finitely many values. The integrals then become finite sums, which can always be evaluated. Models of this type are known as hidden Markov models.
 
\begin{example}[Exact filtering for the drone model]\label{ex: drone exact solution}
The drone model of Section~\ref{sec: state space model} has the linear transition model \eqref{eq:drone transition} and the linear observation model \eqref{eq: drone observation}, and both noise terms are Gaussian. It is therefore a linear Gaussian state space model, and the filtering distribution of the drone's position and velocity can be computed exactly, without evaluating any integrals. Chapter~\ref{chap:kalman} derives the explicit equations and applies them to this model.
\end{example}

\section{Advantages and disadvantages of Bayesian filtering}
The Bayesian filtering framework has several attractive properties, and it also faces practical challenges. The following summary is based on \cite[Sections~1.4 and~2.2]{sarkka:bayesian_filtering}.

The main strength of the framework is that it treats state estimation consistently as a problem of probability. All knowledge about the state is expressed as a distribution, so every estimate carries its own uncertainty, and this uncertainty is propagated through time. The filtering equations are recursive, so the estimate is updated as new observations arrive without storing the entire measurement history, which makes the method suitable for online use. The framework is also general. Linear and non-linear as well as Gaussian and non-Gaussian models all fit the same two equations, and the filters of the following chapters arise from them as exact solutions or approximations.

The main difficulty is computational. The prediction and normalization integrals are intractable for general models, so everything beyond the special cases of Section~\ref{sec: exact solutions in bayesian filtering} requires approximations, and these approximations introduce errors of their own. The quality of the estimates also depends on the model. If the assumed dynamics or noise levels are wrong, the filter can produce biased or even divergent estimates, a point we return to in Chapter~\ref{chap:kalman} when we study the effect of the noise covariances $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{R}$.
