\subsection{Introduction to Bayesian filtering}
Bayesian filtering provides a structured way to estimate the state of a system that changes over time and can only be observed through noisy data. The system’s state typically consists of time-dependent variables that describe its condition at each point in time. However, measurements are often inaccurate because of noise, which creates uncertainty. On top of that, the system’s behavior over time can be affected by random changes or errors in the model, known as process noise.
\\
Bayesian filtering uses all observations up to the current time to estimate the system’s current state. Bayesian smoothing goes a step further by also using future observations to get better estimates of past states. Both filtering and smoothing are key tools in time series analysis and are used in many different fields. The following sections introduce the principles of Bayesian inference and show how they lead to the recursive Bayesian filtering equations.

\subsection{Bayesian inference} % placement to be determine
Bayesian inference is a \textcolor{orange}{important?} idea: it turns learning into a process of updating probabilities. Every time new data arrive, the prior belief becomes the new posterior, which then serves as the prior for the next update. This recursive idea is the foundation of Bayesian filtering, where we use new sensor data to continuously improve our estimates of a system’s changing state.
The idea: Bayesian inference provides a formal framework for updating our beliefs as new evidence becomes available. Answers an intuitive question: "Given what I already know and what I have just observed, what should I now believe?" This process is how we naturally \textcolor{orange}{learn}. 

\begin{example}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Prior Belief}: Let's look at a self-driving car navigating through a city. Its internal map (prior knowledge) tells the car that the upcoming road is a straight two-lane highway.Its prior belief is that it should stay in its lane and maintain speed. 
    \item \textbf{New Evidence}: Suddenly, the car's sensors, e.g., the cameras, detect a large stationary object in its lane about 100 meters ahead. This is the new data. The likelihood function \textcolor{orange}{evaluates} this new evidence: "What is the probability of seeing this sensor reading if the road is clear (very low) and what is the probability if there is a real obstacle (very high)?" 
    \item \textbf{Updated Belief}: The car combines its initial map knowledge with the new sensor data. This results in a new updated belief. The prior belief of a clear road is replaced by the posterior belief of an obstructed road, triggering an immediate action. Based on this, the car makes a decision to slow down and safely change lanes. 
\end{enumerate}
\end{example}
Bayesian inference formalizes this intuitive process of learning using probability to represent uncertainty. 

An important tool for this framework is Bayes' Theorem. It provides a simple formula to update probabilities. The Theorem is: 
\[P(\theta \mid D) = \frac{P(D\mid \theta) \cdot P(\theta)}{P(D)}\]

Let's look at each component: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Posterior Probability $P(\theta \mid D)$}: This is what we want to compute with the Bayesian analysis. It represents our updated belief about the parameter $\theta$ after seeing the data $D$. It answers the question: "How probable are the values of $\theta$ after seeing the new evidence?"
    \item \textbf{\textcolor{orange}{Likelihood} $P(D \mid \theta)$}: The likelihood expresses how probable the observed data $D$ is, assuming a specific value of the parameter $\theta$. The question it answers is "How likely is it to observe this data if the parameter takes a particular value?"
    \item \textbf{\textcolor{orange}{Prior Probability} $P(\theta)$}: This term shows our belief about $\theta$ before we see the data $D$. If we have little prior information, we can use a vague prior.
    \item \textbf{Marginal Likelihood $P(D)$}: This is the total probability of the data under all possible values of $\theta$. It acts as a normalizing constant that ensures the posterior distribution is a valid probability distribution, i.e., it sums (discrete case) or integrates (continuous case) to 1. \textcolor{orange}{In practice, it can be computationally challenging to calculate, but for many applications, it is treated as a scaling factor}. 
\end{enumerate}

A more intuitive way to write the theorem is: 

\[\text{Posterior} \propto \text{Likelihood } \times \text{ Prior} \]

The symbol $\propto$ means "is proportional to". This tells us that our updated belief (the posterior) is a combination of what we thought before (the prior) and what the data is \textcolor{orange}{telling} us (the likelihood)

\subsection{Bayesian filtering equations}
In a probabilistic state space model, $\{\mathbf{x}_t\}_{t \geq 0}$ denote the sequence of hidden states and let $\{y_t\}_{t \geq 1}$ denote the sequence of observed measurements. The model is defined by: 

\begin{itemize}
    \item The \textbf{initial distribution} $p(\mathbf{x}_0)$,
    \item The \textbf{state transition model} $p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1})$,
    \item The \textbf{observation model} $p(\mathbf{y}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$.
\end{itemize}

Given a sequence of observations $\mathbf{y}_{1:t} = \{\mathbf{y}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{y}_t\}$, the goal of Bayesian filtering is to recursively compute the posterior distribution of the current state:
\[
p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t}).
\]

Bayes filtering applies Bayes' theorem recursively over time within a state space model, allowing us to estimate evolving system states as new data become available.

The following theorem is taken from \cite[Thm.~X]{sarkka:bayesian_filtering}.

\begin{theorem}[Bayesian filtering equations, \cite{sarkka:bayesian_filtering} Thm.~X]
The recursive equations (the Bayesian filter) for computing the predicted distribution $p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1})$ and the filtering distribution $p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t})$ at the time step $t$ are given by the following Bayesian filtering equations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Initialization} The recursion starts from the prior distribution $p(\mathbf{x}_0)$.
    \item \textbf{Prediction step} The predictive distribution of the state $\mathbf{x}_t$ at the time $t$, given the dynamic model, can be computed by the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation
    \[p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}) = \int p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1})p(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1})d\mathbf{x}_{t-1}.\]
    \item \textbf{Update step} Given the measurement $\mathbf{y}_t$ at time step $t$ the posterior distribution of the state $\mathbf{x}_t$ can be computed by Bayes' rule
    \[p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t}) = \frac{1}{Z_t}\textbf{ }p(\mathbf{y}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_t)\textbf{ }p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}),\]
    where the normalization constant $Z_t$ is given as 
    \[Z_t = \int p(\mathbf{y}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_t) \textbf{ }p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}) \textbf{ }d\mathbf{x}_t\]
\end{itemize}
The integrals are replaced with summations if the components of the state are discrete. 
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For the proof, check pages 55 and 56 in the book `Bayesian filtering and smoothing' by Simo Särkkä. 
\end{proof}

%{In principle}, Bayesian filtering provides a framework for sequentially estimating uncertain quantities as new data arrives. 

\begin{example}[Estimating the Drone’s Position with Bayesian Filtering]
Continuing from Example~1.4, the state of the drone consists of its position and velocity, while the GPS provides noisy position measurements. The goal of Bayesian filtering is to combine what we know about the drone's movement with the new observations we receive at each time step. At each time step $t$, we want to compute the posterior distribution of the current state given all observations up to that point, $p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t})$, which represents our best estimate of where the drone is and how fast it is moving.

\noindent Bayesian filtering works in two main steps: \textbf{prediction} and \textbf{update}. 

In the prediction step, we use the transition model 
\[
\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \mathbf{q}_{t-1},
\]
to guess where the next state will be based on the previous one. The term $\mathbf{q}_{t-1}$ represents small random changes such as wind that can push the drone slightly off course. This step gives us a predicted state, our belief, about the drone’s position before seeing the new GPS data. 

In the update step, we correct this prediction using the new observation $\mathbf{y}_t$. According to Bayes' rule, 
\[
p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t}) \propto
p(\mathbf{y}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_t)\, p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}),
\]
where the likelihood term $p(\mathbf{y}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$ comes from the observation model
\[
\mathbf{y}_t = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{r}_t.
\]
This step adjusts our guess by taking the measurement into account and reduces uncertainty about the drone's true position. \\

Intuitively, the prediction uses our knowledge about how the drone moves, while the update adjusts this prediction according to the noisy GPS data. Repeating these steps over time provides a continuous estimate of the drone’s trajectory. In the special case where the models are linear and the noise is Gaussian, this method becomes the \emph{Kalman filter}, which will be discussed in Chapter~3.
\end{example}


\subsection{Exact solutions in Bayesian filtering}

% \st{In theory, Bayesian filtering works for any state space model. But in practice, we usually can't compute the exact posterior distribution because the required integrals are too difficult or do not have closed-form solutions. Exact solutions to the Bayesian filtering problem are only possible under restrictive conditions. 

% The conditions: }
% \begin{itemize} 
%     \item \textbf{Linear system}: transition and measurement are linear in the state. 
%     \item \textbf{Gaussian noise}: process and measurement noise are normally distributed. 
% \end{itemize}
In practice, the integrals in the Bayesian filtering equations are rarely manageable, so we must rely on approximations. Exact solutions are only possible under restrictive assumptions, specifically, when the system is linear and the noise is Gaussian.
% \st{Under these conditions the filtering equations lead to closed-form solutions.}
Under these assumptions, the Bayesian filtering equations simplify to closed-form expressions. 

\begin{example}[Exact Filtering for the Drone Model]
Let us return to the drone example from the previous sections. The state of the drone contains its position and velocity, and the measurements come from the GPS sensor. Both the state transition and observation models are linear, and the noise is Gaussian:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{x}_t &= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \mathbf{q}_{t-1}, \quad
\mathbf{q}_{t-1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{Q}), \\
\mathbf{y}_t &= \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{r}_t, \quad
\mathbf{r}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{R}).
\end{aligned}
\]

Because this system satisfies the linear and Gaussian assumptions, the Bayesian filtering equations can be solved exactly using the Kalman filter. The filter gives a step by step equations that update the mean and the uncertainty (the covariance) of the drone's estimated state at each time step. In other words, it allows us to estimate the drone's position and velocity gradually as new GPS data arrives without the need to solve difficult integrals. The next chapter will show the explicit equations of the Kalman filter and how they are applied to this drone model.
\end{example}


\subsection{Advantages and Disadvantages of Bayesian Filtering}
% Chapter 1, Section 1.4 and Chapter 2, Section 2.2 - \cite{sarkka:bayesian_filtering}
Although the Bayesian filtering framework offers several attractive properties, it also faces practical challenges. The main advantages and disadvantages are summarized below, following the explanation by Simo Särkkä in `Bayesian filtering and smoothing' \cite{sarkka:bayesian_filtering}. 

\subsubsection*{Advantages of Bayesian Filtering}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Principled probabilistic framework:} Bayesian filtering formulates the estimation problem in a consistent probabilistic manner. All information about the state of the system is expressed as a probability distribution, allowing the uncertainty in the estimates to be explicitly represented and propagated through time.

    \item \textbf{Recursive computation:} The Bayesian filtering equations are recursive, meaning the state estimate can be updated sequentially as new observations arrive. This property makes the method suitable for online applications, where data are processed in real time without the need to store the entire measurement history.

    \item \textbf{Model flexibility:} The Bayesian framework is highly general and can be applied to a wide range of systems, including both linear and nonlinear, as well as Gaussian and non-Gaussian models. Various commonly used filters, such as the Kalman filter, extended Kalman filter (EKF), unscented Kalman filter (UKF), and particle filter, can all be derived as special cases or approximations of the general Bayesian filtering equations.

    \item \textbf{Optimality under given assumptions:} When the assumptions of the model are satisfied (for example, linearity and Gaussian noise in the Kalman filter), the Bayesian filter provides the optimal estimate in the minimum mean-square error sense.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Disadvantages of Bayesian Filtering}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Computational complexity:}  
    The exact Bayesian filtering equations involve high-dimensional integrals that are generally intractable. In practice, approximations are required, which can be computationally expensive and may limit real-time applicability.

    \item \textbf{Dependence on model accuracy:}  
    The quality of the estimates depends critically on the accuracy of the system and measurement models. Incorrect assumptions about the dynamics or noise characteristics can lead to biased or divergent estimates.

    \item \textbf{Approximation errors:}  
    For nonlinear or non-Gaussian systems, analytical solutions are typically unavailable. Approximations such as linearization (in EKF) or sampling (in particle filters) introduce errors that can reduce estimation accuracy.

    \item \textbf{Implementation challenges:}  
    Advanced Bayesian filtering algorithms, particularly particle filters, can be complex to implement and tune. They require careful selection of parameters, such as the number of particles and resampling strategies, to achieve reliable performance.
\end{itemize}
